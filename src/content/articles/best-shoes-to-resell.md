<p>The best shoes to resell are the ones that already have a proven buyer market — not the ones that look impressive on a shelf. After tracking every pair I've bought and sold since 2023, a clear pattern emerged: a handful of brands account for the majority of my sales, and a long list of "maybe" brands mostly just take up space in my inventory. Here's exactly what the data says, broken down by brand, with the real numbers behind it.</p>

      <h2>The brands with the most consistent buyer demand</h2>

      <p>I track every purchase and sale in a spreadsheet — cost, sale price, platform, days to sell, net profit. Across hundreds of pairs, a small group of brands consistently moved the most units and produced reliable profit. But here's the thing — no brand sells itself. Every one of these still has to pass the same checks on condition, size, and price before it's worth your money. Brand just tells you there's a buyer out there. It doesn't guarantee the pair in your hand is the right one.</p>

      <h2>How to evaluate a shoe in 10 seconds</h2>

      <p>Here's the actual process I run through every time I pick up a pair at a thrift store, before I decide whether to buy:</p>

      <p><strong>1. Check the condition first.</strong> Look at the outsole, the material, and check for dirt or stains. A shoe in rough shape isn't worth the cleaning time even if the brand is good.</p>

      <p><strong>2. Find the style code.</strong> The size tag usually has a style or model number. If I don't recognize the shoe, this is what I look up to check recent sold prices before committing.</p>

      <p><strong>3. Compare size, condition, and price together.</strong> A great brand in a bad size, or a great size in bad condition, both change the math. All three factors have to line up before I buy.</p>

      <h2>Does shoe size matter?</h2>

      <p>Yes. Size affects how fast a pair sells, regardless of brand. The sizes that move fastest in my experience are men's 9 through 13 and women's 7 through 10 — these cover the largest share of buyers. That doesn't mean other sizes never sell, but if you're choosing between two similar pairs, size is a real tiebreaker.</p>

      <h2>How much should you actually pay?</h2>

      <p>There's no fixed ceiling on what to pay for a pair — it's entirely about the return on investment. I won't spend $100 to sell a shoe for $125. That math doesn't justify the time and risk. But I'll spend $100 on a pair of boots I'm confident will sell for $200 to $300, because the margin and the brand history support it.</p>

      <p>This is exactly why knowing your brands matters so much. The same $7 price tag at Goodwill can be a $15 profit or a $230 profit, depending entirely on what's actually sitting in front of you.</p>

      <h2>Brands to walk past</h2>

      <p>Just as important as knowing what to buy is knowing what to leave on the shelf. Mall brands and big-box store brands generally don't carry resale value worth your time — the exception is when you find them brand new with tags, since new condition changes the math even on a so-so brand. Walmart and Target private-label brands fall into the same category. These aren't disasters to pick up for a dollar, but they're not where your sourcing time should go.</p>

      <p>The shortcut is this: if you don't recognize the brand and it's not clearly high-quality construction, don't guess. Look it up before you buy, or pass and keep moving. There are always more shoes on the next rack.</p>

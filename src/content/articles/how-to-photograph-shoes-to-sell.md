<p>To photograph shoes for sale, you need consistent lighting, a clean background, and a repeatable sequence of angles that covers what buyers actually check before they commit. That's it. The gear can be modest — my current setup cost under $200 total — but it needs to be set up the same way every time so you're not solving the same lighting problem on every shoot. Here's exactly what I do, from the moment I sit down with a pair to the moment the photos are uploaded.</p>

<h2>The setup</h2>

<p>I shoot on a white background — a large foam board propped against the wall works fine. The shoes sit on top of an 18-inch lazy Susan so I can rotate between angles without picking the shoe up and disturbing the frame. That single change — adding the turntable — cut my per-pair photo time significantly. Before I had it, I was repositioning the shoe by hand for every angle, which sounds minor until you're doing it forty times a shoot.</p>

<p>For lighting, I use a two-source setup. The primary light is a softbox kit — I use the <a href="https://amzn.to/3TiwIB4" rel="nofollow">Torjim Softbox Lighting Kit</a>, positioned to the side of the shooting area at roughly 45 degrees. This gives soft, even light across the shoe without the harsh hotspots you get from overhead overhead ceiling fixtures or direct flash. Before I got the softbox, I tried natural window light. It works on overcast days but it's inconsistent — color shifts hour to hour, contrast changes, and you can't shoot at night. The softbox fixed all of that.</p>

<p>The secondary light is a ring light — I use the <a href="https://amzn.to/44L606E" rel="nofollow">UBeesize Ring Light</a>, mounted on its included tripod and positioned directly overhead, pointing straight down at the shoe. Its job is fill: eliminating the shadows the softbox casts on the underside of the shoe and in the toe box. The ring light was actually my first lighting upgrade from natural light. I used it alone for a while. It's decent by itself, but once I added the softbox as primary, the quality jumped significantly. Now it earns its keep as overhead fill.</p>

<p>The lazy Susan I use is the <a href="https://amzn.to/4wpIBDW" rel="nofollow">Lipper International 18" Acacia Lazy Susan</a>. Eighteen inches is the right size — most shoes fit comfortably, including larger men's boots, and there's enough surface to get both shoes in frame for the front and back shots without looking cramped.</p>

![Overhead shot of a phone tripod, ring light, and lazy Susan turntable set up for shoe photography](/articles/photography/photography-setup.jpg)
*A simple setup: softbox lighting, ring light fill, and lazy Susan turntable.*

<p>One detail that matters more than it sounds: I keep this setup standing. I don't break it down between shoots. The softbox stays in the corner, the ring light stays on its tripod, the lazy Susan stays on the table. The reason is friction. If I have to set the whole thing up each time, I delay the shoot. If it's already up, I can knock out photos for a pair in under five minutes.</p>

<h2>Preparing the shoe before you shoot</h2>

<p>A good photo of a dirty shoe is still a bad listing photo. Before anything goes on the lazy Susan, I clean it — quick wipe of the uppers with a damp cloth, scrub the soles with a stiff brush if there's grit in the lugs, and spot-clean any marks on the midsole. Nothing elaborate. The goal is to make the shoe look like what it is: a used shoe in whatever condition it's actually in. Hiding scuffs doesn't help anyone — buyers will notice on arrival and open a return. Show the truth clearly.</p>

<p>After cleaning, I insert shoe trees. I use the <a href="https://amzn.to/4hcA4Qb" rel="nofollow">Angelus Adjustable Shoe Trees</a> for every shoot. They hold the shoe's shape open so it photographs full and true to form rather than collapsed at the toe box or with a wrinkled vamp. Before I used shoe trees, I stuffed the toe with packing paper. It works in a pinch, but the result isn't as clean — the paper can show through mesh uppers, and the shape isn't as consistent. Shoe trees take two seconds to insert and make every photo look more professional.</p>

<p>I shoot both shoes. Both shoes go in the frame for the front shot and back shot. For all the profile and close-up shots, I pick one shoe — typically the left — and keep it consistent. eBay doesn't require both shoes in every frame, and a cluttered shot with two shoes at odd angles is harder to read than a single clean profile.</p>

<h2>The shot sequence</h2>

<p>I shoot the same ten angles every time, in the same order. Consistency matters here: if your listings all follow the same photo sequence, buyers can find what they're looking for fast. That reduces friction and it reduces questions.</p>

<p><strong>Front shot.</strong> Both shoes on the lazy Susan, toes pointed toward the camera, square to the frame. This is the photo that usually becomes the cover image on eBay. It shows the overall silhouette and colorway.</p>

![Altra Olympus 5 photographed straight-on from the front](/articles/photography/altra-olympus-front.jpeg)
*Front shot — square to the camera, both shoes visible.*

<p><strong>Back shot.</strong> Rotate the lazy Susan 180 degrees. Both shoes still in frame, now showing the heel. This angle reveals the heel counter shape and any wear at the back — buyers with specific fit needs pay attention to this shot.</p>

![Altra Olympus 5 photographed from the back](/articles/photography/altra-olympus-back.jpeg)
*Back shot — shows heel shape and any wear at the counter.*

<p><strong>Left profile.</strong> One shoe now, left side facing the camera. This is the shot most buyers study — full lateral view of the upper, midsole stack height, and the overall profile of the shoe's geometry.</p>

![Altra Olympus 5 left profile view](/articles/photography/altra-olympus-left-profile.jpeg)
*Left profile.*

<p><strong>Right profile.</strong> Rotate 180 on the lazy Susan. Same shoe, opposite side. Some shoes have asymmetric logos or lace configurations that only appear on one side.</p>

![Altra Olympus 5 right profile view](/articles/photography/altra-olympus-right-profile.jpeg)
*Right profile.*

<p><strong>Overhead heel.</strong> Camera positioned directly above the shoe, angled toward the heel. This shows the tread pattern at the back of the sole and gives a clear read of rear heel wear. Trail runners and hiking shoes in particular get scrutinized here — buyers want to know how much tread is left and whether the wear pattern is even.</p>

![Altra Olympus 5 overhead shot focused on the heel](/articles/photography/altra-olympus-overhead-heel.jpeg)
*Overhead heel shot — shows tread wear pattern at the back of the shoe.*

<p><strong>Overhead toe.</strong> Shift the frame forward. Same overhead angle, now focused on the toe box area. Shows tread wear at the front and the toe box shape. On road runners, buyers check here for creasing or separation at the toecap.</p>

![Altra Olympus 5 overhead shot focused on the toe box](/articles/photography/altra-olympus-overhead-toe.jpeg)
*Overhead toe shot — shows tread wear and toe shape.*

<p><strong>Interior.</strong> Shoe off the lazy Susan, held open to show the insole. This shot matters more than most sellers think. Buyers want to see insole condition — is the foam compressed? Is there staining? Does the heel lining look intact? A clean interior is a selling point. A worn one is still fine to show — just show it accurately. Hiding interior wear leads to returns.</p>

![Interior view of the Altra Olympus 5 showing the insole and lining](/articles/photography/altra-olympus-interier.jpeg)
*Interior shot — buyers want to see insole condition and any staining.*

<p><strong>Soles.</strong> Shoe flipped, both soles facing the camera or one flat on the surface with the camera overhead. This is the single most-checked photo for used shoe buyers. Tread depth is the primary signal of how much life is left in the shoe. Shoot this one well — sharp, well-lit, no motion blur.</p>

![Close-up of the outsoles on the Altra Olympus 5](/articles/photography/altra-olympus-soles.jpeg)
*Sole shot — the single most-checked photo for used shoe buyers.*

<p><strong>Size tag.</strong> Close-up of the size label inside the shoe. I include this on every single listing. It confirms size, width, and sometimes country of manufacture — all things buyers check before purchasing, and all things that generate messages if they can't see them in the photos. A clear size tag shot eliminates those questions.</p>

![Close-up of the size tag inside the Altra Olympus 5](/articles/photography/altra-olympus-size-tag.jpg)
*Size tag close-up — confirms size and width without buyers having to ask.*

<p>Ten shots per pair, same sequence every time. On eBay, you get up to 24 photos. I don't use all 24 — ten is enough to show a used shoe honestly. More photos aren't always better if they're redundant angles of the same information. What matters is that each shot answers a distinct question a buyer might have.</p>

<h2>Getting photos from phone to listing</h2>

<p>I shoot with my phone. I'm not going to pretend a camera would produce better results — it would — but the phone-to-listing workflow is fast and I haven't found the difference to be meaningful for the price points I'm selling at. A $55 pair of HOKA doesn't need studio-quality photography. It needs ten clear, well-lit shots that load fast and show what the shoe looks like.</p>

<p>After the shoot, I AirDrop the photos directly to my laptop where I have the eBay draft open. I don't edit them. Consistent studio lighting means the images are already accurate to the shoe's actual colors and condition. Heavy editing — boosting brightness, cranking saturation — creates a gap between what buyers see in photos and what arrives in the mail. That gap is how you end up with returns.</p>

<p>If something needs cropping, I crop it. That's it. The goal is fast and accurate, not polished.</p>

<h2>What slows most sellers down</h2>

<p>The two things I see slow sellers down on photos are inconsistency and perfectionism, and they're almost opposites.</p>

<p>Inconsistency means a different background each shoot, different lighting depending on where in the house you set up, no fixed sequence of angles so you're improvising every time. The fix is a permanent setup you don't break down and a sequence you follow without thinking.</p>

<p>Perfectionism means spending thirty minutes per pair trying to get a magazine-quality shot of a $40 thrift store find. The math doesn't work. Your photos need to be clear and accurate. They do not need to be beautiful. The shoe sells because the price is right and the condition is honestly represented — the photos just need to prove it.</p>

<p>Set your space up once, shoot the same way every time, and stop second-guessing frames that are already good enough. The bottleneck in most reselling operations isn't the quality of photos — it's the volume of listings. Fast and consistent beats slow and perfect.</p>

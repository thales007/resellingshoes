Flipping shoes means buying a pair for less than you can sell it for online — and doing it repeatedly. That's the whole business. You find a pair of New Balance or Merrell at a thrift store for $8, list it on eBay for $55, sell it for $46 net after fees and shipping, and put that money back into the next pair. Do that consistently and it becomes a real side income. This guide covers exactly how to do it, from the first sourcing trip to the first sale.

## What "flipping shoes" actually means

Shoe flipping is not about hype sneakers or waiting in line for limited releases. The everyday shoe flip is simpler and more consistent than that. You're looking for quality brands — performance running shoes, hiking boots, work boots, leather dress shoes — at thrift stores and yard sales where they're priced at a fraction of their resale value. The people donating them don't know what they're worth online. You do. That information gap is where the profit lives.

The brands that flip consistently are the ones with large, active buyer bases: New Balance, HOKA, Brooks, On Running, Danner, Frye, Ariat, Allen Edmonds, Merrell, KEEN, Salomon. These are not fashion brands or status symbols — they're shoes people buy because they work, and people search for specific models when they need a replacement or want a deal on a quality pair.

My target is a minimum of $20 net profit per pair. If the source price is low and the shoes are in excellent condition, I'll take $15. It's not about hitting a home run on every flip — it's about hitting singles and doubles consistently across volume. Fifty pairs at $20 net is $1,000. That's the math behind the business.

Before buying any pair, I run a quick mental calculation: source price + platform fees (~13.25% on eBay) + shipping = total cost. If the sold comps on eBay support a price that clears $20 net after all of that, I buy it. If they don't, I put it back.

## Where to find shoes to flip

**Yard sales** are the best source for low prices. On Saturday mornings I check Facebook buy-and-sell groups for listings, plan a route, and focus on neighborhood-wide sales where I can cover many sellers in one stop. Prices at yard sales are often $1–$5 per pair — the margins on a good find can be significant.

**Thrift stores** — Goodwill, Salvation Army, Savers — are more consistent than yard sales but priced slightly higher, usually $5–$15 per pair. I hit a few stores once a week on a set schedule. Inventory turns over regularly, so showing up consistently means catching new arrivals before other resellers do.

**The key in both places is speed.** You need to evaluate a pair in under a minute. Pick it up, check the brand, look at the condition, bend the sole to check for cracks, find the style code on the size tag, and make a call. If you're not sure, look up the sold comps on eBay before you buy. If the math works, grab it. If it doesn't, move on.

## How to evaluate a pair before you buy

Not every shoe with a good brand name is worth flipping. Before committing to any pair, run through this checklist:

**Brand first.** Is this a brand with an active resale market? New Balance, HOKA, Brooks, On Running, Merrell, KEEN, Salomon, Danner, Frye, Ariat, Allen Edmonds — yes. Mall brands, Walmart or Target private labels, heavily worn fashion brands — usually no.

**Condition second.** Bend the sole — if it cracks, walk away. Check the midsole for separation. Look for stains, tears, or heavy heel drag that won't clean up. Smell for smoke — it almost never comes out. A great brand in bad condition is still a bad buy.

**Size third.** Men's 9–13 and women's 7–10 move the fastest. Other sizes sell too, but slower. Size is a real factor in how long your money sits in inventory before you get it back.

**Price fourth.** Check sold comps on eBay before you buy anything you're uncertain about. Search the brand and model, filter to sold listings, and see what buyers have actually paid — not what sellers are asking. If the math supports a $20+ net profit, buy it. If it doesn't, leave it.

## How to list a shoe for maximum visibility

Your listing title is the most important part of the process. eBay matches buyer searches against your title — every word you include is a potential search match. Use this format every time: **Brand + Model + Style Code + Gender + Size + Key Details + Condition**.

Fill in every item specific eBay asks for: brand, model, US size, UK size, EU size, gender, color, material, condition. These fields drive search traffic in ways your title alone can't. The more complete your listing, the more places it shows up.

Price at the high end of recent sold comps and enable Best Offer. This gives you negotiation room while keeping the door open for buyers who'll pay full price. I always use fixed price, never auction — a shoe in a specific size has a specific pool of buyers, and that buyer might not be searching today. Fixed price keeps the listing live until they find it.

## The difference between someone who flips once and someone who builds a business

Most people who try shoe flipping quit before they figure it out. They go to the thrift store a few times, buy some pairs that sit for months, and conclude it doesn't work. Usually the problem isn't the business — it's one of three things.

**Wrong brands.** Buying brands that don't have an active buyer market ties up your capital in inventory that doesn't move. Stick to the brands with proven demand until you know the market well enough to branch out.

**No system.** Flipping shoes once in a while produces inconsistent results. A weekly sourcing routine, consistent listing, and tracking your sales are what separate someone who makes occasional money from someone who clears $500–$1,000 a month reliably.

**Slow inventory.** A shoe that takes six months to sell isn't a good flip even if the margin is decent. Your money is tied up the whole time. Target inventory that sells within 60–90 days — that's what keeps capital moving and the business growing.

The flip itself is simple. The system around it is what makes it sustainable.

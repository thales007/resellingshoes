Best Shoes to Resell: What Actually Sells at the Thrift Store

By Timothy Hales  ·  ResellingShoes.com

The best shoes to resell are the ones that already have a proven buyer market — not the ones that look impressive on a shelf. After tracking every pair I've bought and sold since 2023, a clear pattern emerged: a handful of brands account for the majority of my sales, and a long list of "maybe" brands mostly just take up space in my inventory. Here's exactly what the data says, broken down by brand, with the real numbers behind it.

The brands with the most consistent buyer demand

I track every purchase and sale in a spreadsheet — cost, sale price, platform, days to sell, net profit. Across hundreds of pairs, a small group of brands consistently moved the most units and produced reliable profit. But here's the thing — no brand sells itself. Every one of these still has to pass the same checks on condition, size, and price before it's worth your money. Brand just tells you there's a buyer out there. It doesn't guarantee the pair in your hand is the right one.

High-confidence brands

Strong, consistent buyer demand

New Balance HOKA Brooks On Running Altra Danner Frye Ariat Allen Edmonds

These brands show up across my own sales data and across other resellers' numbers — large buyer bases that search and buy year-round, regardless of trends. On Running in particular has grown fast over the past couple of years; if you don't usually stop for it, it's worth starting. Boots and dress shoes from Danner, Frye, Ariat, and Allen Edmonds carry higher price points, which means a strong margin even at lower volume — the Allen Edmonds penny loafer example below is a good case of that. Even with a brand this reliable, a damaged sole or a bad size still kills the flip. Check the shoe, not just the logo.

The biggest surprise in my own data wasn't a sneaker brand — it was Capezio, a dance shoe brand. Most resellers walk right past the dance section. I don't. Specialty categories like dance, cycling, and work boots can be sleepers because there's less reseller competition sourcing them, but the buyer demand is just as real.

Brands worth knowing, even if you buy them less often

These brands don't move in the same volume as the list above, but when you find the right pair in the right condition, the profit per pair is often higher. This is where actually knowing your brands pays off — recognizing a specific model, checking sold prices, and understanding which of these are worth a closer look before you commit.

Know your model

Strong profit, more selective buying

Nike Adidas ASICS Saucony Salomon Dr. Martens Birkenstock Cole Haan Sam Edelman Under Armour Ecco Wolverine Dansko Vans Converse Crocs Skechers KEEN

Nike and Adidas have the largest overall buyer base of any brand, but that also means heavy competition from other resellers and, on certain styles, an oversaturated market — general releases often sit at or near retail after fees. ASICS has grown fast in resale demand recently, but I don't source it often enough to call it a sure thing yet. Crocs, Skechers, and KEEN have performed well for me personally — your local market may vary, so watch your own sold data once you start tracking it.

"I look at the bottom of the shoe, the material, whether it's dirty or stained. Then I check the size tag for the style code so I can look up sold prices before I decide."

How to evaluate a shoe in 10 seconds

Here's the actual process I run through every time I pick up a pair at a thrift store, before I decide whether to buy:

1. Check the condition first. Look at the outsole, the material, and check for dirt or stains. A shoe in rough shape isn't worth the cleaning time even if the brand is good.

2. Find the style code. The size tag usually has a style or model number. If I don't recognize the shoe, this is what I look up to check recent sold prices before committing.

3. Compare size, condition, and price together. A great brand in a bad size, or a great size in bad condition, both change the math. All three factors have to line up before I buy.

What automatically disqualifies a shoe

Even a great brand isn't worth buying if the shoe fails any of these checks. Bend the shoe to test the sole — if it cracks or the midsole is separating from the upper, walk away. Beyond that, watch for:

Automatic pass, regardless of brand

✕ Cracked soles or visible midsole separation
✕ Heavy heel drag or uneven wear
✕ Stains that won't realistically clean out
✕ Tears or holes in the upper material
✕ Smoke smell — this almost never comes out

Does shoe size matter?

Yes. Size affects how fast a pair sells, regardless of brand. The sizes that move fastest in my experience are men's 9 through 13 and women's 7 through 10 — these cover the largest share of buyers. That doesn't mean other sizes never sell, but if you're choosing between two similar pairs, size is a real tiebreaker.

How much should you actually pay?

There's no fixed ceiling on what to pay for a pair — it's entirely about the return on investment. I won't spend $100 to sell a shoe for $125. That math doesn't justify the time and risk. But I'll spend $100 on a pair of boots I'm confident will sell for $200 to $300, because the margin and the brand history support it.

This is exactly why knowing your brands matters so much. The same $7 price tag at Goodwill can be a $15 profit or a $230 profit, depending entirely on what's actually sitting in front of you.

Brands to walk past

Just as important as knowing what to buy is knowing what to leave on the shelf. Mall brands and big-box store brands generally don't carry resale value worth your time — the exception is when you find them brand new with tags, since new condition changes the math even on a so-so brand. Walmart and Target private-label brands fall into the same category. These aren't disasters to pick up for a dollar, but they're not where your sourcing time should go.

The shortcut is this: if you don't recognize the brand and it's not clearly high-quality construction, don't guess. Look it up before you buy, or pass and keep moving. There are always more shoes on the next rack.
